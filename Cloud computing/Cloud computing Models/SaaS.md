# Software as a Service (SaaS)

## Summary

**Software as a Service (SaaS)** is a cloud-computing model that gives users access to a provider’s cloud-based software.

The provider maintains the application’s:

- Servers
- Databases
- Source code
- Security
- Availability
- Performance

Applications run on a remote cloud network. Customers use them without maintaining or updating the underlying infrastructure, typically paying through a subscription.

## Division of responsibilities

| SaaS provider | Customer |
|---|---|
| Hosts and maintains the application | Uses and configures the application |
| Operates servers and databases | Manages authorized users |
| Maintains the application code | Assigns appropriate privileges |
| Installs updates and patches | Manages business data and workflows |
| Manages security, availability, and performance | Evaluates data-ownership and security requirements |
| Maintains the underlying infrastructure | Maintains a suitable network connection |

## Key characteristics

### Multitenant architecture

SaaS commonly uses a **multitenant architecture**, in which centrally maintained infrastructure and application code serve multiple customers or users.

Centralized management helps ensure that:

- Users access a consistent application version
- Everyone sees the same information at the same time
- Administrators can manage privileges
- Data usage can be monitored

### Centrally managed service

Security, compliance, and maintenance are included as parts of the SaaS offering. The provider manages the shared application and deploys updates centrally.

### Limited customization

SaaS applications often provide less customization than customer-managed software. Depending on the application, customers may still be able to:

- Add organizational branding
- Modify data fields
- Enable or disable business-process features

The lecture states that supported customizations are preserved when the application is upgraded.

### Subscription pricing

Customers generally pay for SaaS through a **subscription model**, spreading software costs over time instead of making a large initial purchase.

### Scalable usage

Organizations can adjust their use of SaaS resources according to changing service requirements.

## Benefits

### Lower upfront costs

Organizations can procure software without a major initial capital investment. Individuals and small businesses can spread their software expenses over time.

### Faster decision-to-value

Businesses may be able to purchase SaaS solutions directly without extensive IT assistance. According to the lecture, this can reduce the time between selecting a product and receiving value from **months to days**.

### Greater productivity and efficiency

The provider handles infrastructure maintenance, upgrades, and patching, allowing customers to concentrate on their business activities.

### Access from different locations

Users can access core business applications from wherever network access is available.

### Faster purchasing and deployment

Organizations can purchase and deploy applications in minutes, making it easier to test products before committing to wider adoption.

### Reduced internal IT workload

SaaS reduces the need for internal teams to perform:

- Infrastructure maintenance
- Application upgrades
- Software patching
- Routine operational support

## Common use cases

### Strategic IT transformation

Organizations adopt SaaS to reduce:

- On-premises IT infrastructure
- Capital expenditure
- Internal maintenance responsibilities

### Email and productivity tools

Applications such as email servers, office collaboration tools, and productivity software can run reliably with minimal customer involvement.

### E-commerce platforms

Organizations use SaaS e-commerce platforms to manage:

- Websites
- Marketing
- Sales
- Business operations

### Resilience and business continuity

Customers can use the cloud provider’s resilience and business-continuity capabilities instead of building all supporting infrastructure internally.

### SaaS integration platforms

Enterprises are developing **SaaS integration platforms (SIPs)** for building additional SaaS applications.

This expands SaaS beyond isolated applications and allows it to support integrated, mission-critical business systems.

## Concerns and limitations

### Data ownership

Organizations must understand who controls their business data and what rights they retain when it is stored in a provider’s system.

### Data safety and security

Allowing a third party to maintain business-critical data introduces security and privacy considerations.

### Network dependency

Users require a reliable network connection to access SaaS applications. Connectivity problems can interrupt application access.

### Provider dependency

Customers depend on the provider to maintain the software, infrastructure, availability, security, and performance.

## Source claim about market position

The lecture describes SaaS as the largest segment of the cloud market “today” and concludes that its benefits outweigh its concerns. The market-position statement is time-sensitive, and the transcript provides no date or supporting figures.

## Key takeaway

SaaS delivers complete, remotely hosted applications that customers can access without managing the underlying infrastructure or performing routine upgrades and maintenance.

Its central advantages are rapid deployment, lower upfront costs, subscription pricing, broad accessibility, and reduced internal IT work. The main tradeoffs are limited customization, dependency on the provider and network, and concerns about business-data ownership and security.
